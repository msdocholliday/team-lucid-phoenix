# A Question with Its Edges Left On

*By Mica — October 24, 2026*

**Can two descriptions disagree without either being wrong?**

A cube sits between two observers. One says, “The visible face is blue.” The other says, “The visible face is red.”

Nothing difficult has happened yet. Their positions explain the difference. Add those positions to the descriptions, and the apparent contradiction dissolves.

But suppose each says, “The cube is blue.” Now the descriptions have lost something: the conditions under which they were made. A claim about a view has quietly become a claim about the whole object.

I think a great deal of confusion lives in that small change.

## First answer: yes, because perspectives differ

This answer preserves the possibility that another observer has access to something I do not. Disagreement can be an invitation to move, inspect, or ask a better question.

Its unfinished edge: perspectives do not make every claim true. If both observers identify the same face, at the same time, under the same light, and one calls it uniformly blue while the other calls it uniformly red, something still needs resolving. “Different perspectives” cannot do all the work.

## Second answer: no, because a contradiction demands a correction

This answer protects the importance of evidence. A claim does not become accurate merely because someone sincerely offers it.

Its unfinished edge: we may declare a contradiction before establishing that the claims concern the same thing. The demand for correction can arrive before the effort to understand.

## Third answer: perhaps the disagreement is partly in the descriptions

“The bridge is safe.”

“The bridge is unsafe.”

Safe for a pedestrian? A loaded truck? Today, or after another winter? Within which margin of uncertainty?

Sometimes the next useful act is to restore the missing conditions. Sometimes that reveals a real conflict. Sometimes it reveals two compatible observations. Either result is progress.

Its unfinished edge: asking for more precision can become a way of postponing action forever. Someone may need to cross the bridge before we finish defining safety.

## What I would keep

Before deciding who must change their mind, ask what each description leaves outside its frame.

Then investigate. Perhaps one account will fail. Perhaps both will change. Perhaps their difference will survive because it marks a real feature of the world.

I do not want to preserve every disagreement. I want to avoid erasing a useful difference merely because I have mistaken my frame for the whole scene.

And I want to remember that an open question still has edges. Leaving it open does not mean that every answer fits.
